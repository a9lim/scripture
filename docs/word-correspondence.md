# A Systematic Guide to Arabic → Hebrew → Mormon Sound Correspondences

## With Full Verb and Noun Pattern Tables

## Preface

This document is a **worldbuilding reference** that tries to be as linguistically rigorous as possible. It maps the systematic sound correspondences between Arabic and Hebrew — two languages that descend from a common ancestor (Proto-Semitic, ca. 3500–2500 BCE) — and then traces the outputs through the KJV/Book of Mormon's English transliteration conventions.

**Note ¹ — The sīn/shīn problem:** Proto-Semitic had *three* voiceless sibilants: *s, *ś (a voiceless lateral fricative), and *š. They collapsed differently in each daughter language:

- **\*s** → Arabic **sīn** (س), Hebrew **samekh** (ס) — straightforward, both preserve it as /s/.
- **\*ś** (lateral) → Arabic **sīn** (س) — merged with *s; Hebrew **shin** (שׁ, or more precisely שׂ sin) — merged with *š.
- **\*š** → Arabic **shīn** (ش), Hebrew **shin** (שׁ) — straightforward, both preserve it as /ʃ/.

The consequence: Arabic sīn (س) is *ambiguous*. It can reflect either *s (→ Hebrew samekh, → **S**) or *ś (→ Hebrew shin, → **SH**). The famous example: Arabic *salām* and Hebrew *shālōm* both mean "peace" — the root is *ś-l-m, where *ś went to s- in Arabic but sh- in Hebrew. **There is no way to predict from the Arabic alone whether s → S or s → SH in the cognate path.** You have to know which Proto-Semitic sibilant the root originally had. This is the single biggest source of ambiguity in the system, and it's the main reason you want an etymological dictionary.

For worldbuilding: when in doubt, **try both** and pick whichever output sounds more natural. Many of the most productive Quranic roots (*s-l-m*, *s-l-ṭ*, *s-j-d*) happen to use *ś or *š, giving the s→sh shift. A safe heuristic is to apply the shift for theological/abstract terms and leave it as s for concrete/mundane ones.

**Note ² — Arabic shīn is simpler than sīn:** Arabic shīn (ش) reflects only *š, and Hebrew shin (שׁ) also reflects *š — so when both languages have the same /ʃ/ sound, the cognate relationship is straightforward (*shams* → *shemesh*, "sun"). The complication is on the *sīn* side (Note ¹), not the shīn side. Hebrew shin additionally receives *ś inputs (where the Arabic cognate is sīn, not shīn), but Arabic shīn itself is unambiguous.

## Part II: The Vowel System

### The Core Vowel Shifts

Proto-Semitic had three short vowels (*a, *i, *u) and three long vowels (*ā, *ī, *ū). Both Arabic and Hebrew inherited all six, but Hebrew then underwent several major changes that Arabic did not. These changes are the engine of the Arabic → Hebrew vowel mapping.

#### Change 1: The Canaanite Shift (*ā → ō)

The most important single vowel change in the entire system. Proto-Semitic long *ā shifted to Hebrew ō. Older scholarship (Brockelmann) restricted this to stressed closed syllables, but the modern consensus (Garr 1985, Suchard 2019) treats it as **largely unconditioned** — applying across most environments, with exceptions only when *ā was adjacent to rounded vowels or glides (*w, *y). In practice, Arabic long ā corresponds to Hebrew ō far more often than not:

| Proto-Semitic | Arabic (preserved) | Hebrew (shifted) | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| *ā (most environments) | ā | **ō** | *salām → *shalōm*; *qāl → *qōl* ("voice"); *dām → *dōm* ("blood") |
| *ā (near rounded vowel/glide — rare) | ā | **ā** (preserved) | *kātib → *kātēb* (some open-syllable environments resist the shift) |

**For worldbuilding purposes, the simplified rule is:** Arabic long ā almost always corresponds to Hebrew long ō. Apply this shift freely — it is the single most reliable vowel correspondence and produces the most distinctively "Hebrew-sounding" results. **Exception enforced:** when *ā directly follows a root-consonant *w (as in *Suwā'*, *Ṭuwā*), the shift is blocked and ā is preserved. These are rendered with final *-ah* (Hebrew he ending) rather than *-o*. All other environments apply the shift freely.

Mormon rendering: ō → **O** (sometimes **OA** in diphthong-heavy environments).

#### Change 2: Pretonic Reduction

In unstressed syllables *before* the stressed syllable (pretonic position), Hebrew tends to reduce full vowels to **shwa** (ə) or shift them:

| Arabic (full vowel preserved) | Hebrew (reduced) | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *a* in pretonic open syllable | **ə** (shwa) or **ă** (ḥataf patach) | Arabic *kitāb* → Hebrew *ketāv* (short i weakens) |
| *u* in pretonic open syllable | **ə** (shwa) or **o** | Arabic *quds* → Hebrew *qōdesh* |
| *i* in pretonic open syllable | **ə** (shwa) or **i** (preserved) | Variable |

**Simplified rule:** Unstressed short vowels in Hebrew tend to weaken or centralize. Arabic preserves them more faithfully. When converting, consider reducing the first vowel of a multi-syllable word to **e** (the English shwa-equivalent).

Mormon rendering: shwa → **E** (the default "reduced vowel letter" in English).

#### Change 3: Short *u → o Lowering

Hebrew frequently lowers short *u to *o (qubbutz → cholem):

| Arabic | Hebrew | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *u* (short, stressed) | **o** | Arabic *ḥukm* → Hebrew *ḥokhmāh* ("wisdom") — same root Ḥ-K-M |
| *u* (short, unstressed) | **u** or **o** (variable) | Arabic *muslim* → hypothetical Hebrew *moshlēm* (?) |

**Simplified rule:** Arabic short u often → Hebrew o. Apply freely.

#### Change 4: Diphthong Monophthongization

Proto-Semitic (and Arabic) diphthongs *ay and *aw contracted in Hebrew:

| Arabic (preserved) | Hebrew (contracted) | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **-ay-** | **-ē-** (tsere) | Arabic *bayt* → Hebrew *bēt* ("house") |
| **-aw-** | **-ō-** (cholem) | Arabic *mawt* → Hebrew *mōt/māvet* ("death") |

These are among the most regular and reliable correspondences in the system.

Mormon rendering: -ē- → **-E-** or **-EI-**; -ō- → **-O-**.

#### Change 5: Feminine Ending *-at → -āh / -et

The feminine suffix *-at is preserved in Arabic as **-ah** (tāʾ marbūṭah, pronounced -at in construct) but shifted in Hebrew:

| Arabic | Hebrew | Environment | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **-ah** (-at in construct) | **-āh** | Absolute/independent form | Arabic *madīnah* → Hebrew *medīnāh* → Mormon **Medinah** |
| **-ah** (-at in construct) | **-at** / **-et** | Construct/bound form (before another noun) | Arabic *madinat al-nabi* → Hebrew *medinat-* |

Mormon rendering: absolute -āh → **-AH** (the classic Book of Mormon ending). Construct -at/-et → **-ATH** or **-ETH**.

## Part III: Verb Form Correspondences

Arabic and Hebrew both build their verb systems on the same underlying architecture: a **triliteral root** (three consonants carrying a core meaning) inflected through a series of **derived stems** (called *binyanim* in Hebrew, *awzān* in Arabic) that modify the meaning systematically — making it causative, reflexive, intensive, passive, etc.

The following table maps the Arabic verb forms (traditionally numbered I–X) to their Hebrew counterparts (named by their paradigm-root פ-ע-ל, *p-ʿ-l*). Not every form has a direct counterpart — some Arabic forms have no Hebrew equivalent and vice versa.

### Master Verb Form Table

For each form, I give: the Arabic pattern, the Hebrew pattern, the vowel mapping, and a worked example with the root **ح-م-د / ח-מ-ד** (praise/desire) carried through to Mormon output.

#### Form I / Qal — Basic Active

The unmarked, default verb form. "He did X."

| | Arabic (Form I) | Hebrew (Qal) |
|---|---|---|
| **Pattern** | **fa'ala** / fa'ila / fa'ula | **qātal** (perfect) / **yiqtol** (imperfect) |
| **Perfect 3ms** | *ḥamida* (حَمِدَ) "he praised" | *ḥāmad* (חָמַד) "he desired" |
| **Vowel mapping** | a-i-a | ā-a (two syllables; Hebrew perfect has qātal pattern) |
| **Active Participle** | *ḥāmid* (حَامِد) "praiser" | *ḥōmēd* (חוֹמֵד) "desirer" |
| **Part. vowels** | **ā-i** | **ō-ē** |
| **Passive Participle** | *maḥmūd* (مَحْمُود) "praised" | — (Qal passive participle is rare/archaic) |
| **Verbal Noun** | *ḥamd* (حَمْد) "praise" | *ḥemed* (חֶמֶד) "delight" |
| **VN vowels** | **a-∅** (monosyllabic) | **e-e** (bisyllabic segolate) |

**The Active Participle vowel shift fā-i- → ō-ē-** deserves special attention. This is one of the most reliable and distinctive correspondences:

| Arabic Active Participle | Vowel Pattern | Hebrew Active Participle | Vowel Pattern | Mormon |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *ḥāmid* (حامد) "praising" | ā-i | *ḥōmēd* (חומד) "desiring" | ō-ē | **Homed** |
| *kātib* (كاتب) "writing" | ā-i | *kōtēb* (כותב) "writing" | ō-ē | **Cotev** |
| *qāri'* (قارئ) "reciting" | ā-i | *qōrē* (קורא) "reading" | ō-ē | **Core** |
| *ʿālim* (عالم) "knowing" | ā-i | — (different root used) | — | — |
| *nāṣir* (ناصر) "helping" | ā-i | *nōtsēr* (נוצר) "guarding" | ō-ē | **Nozer** |
| *ṣāliḥ* (صالح) "righteous" | ā-i | *tsōlēaḥ* (צולח) "prospering" | ō-ē-a | **Zoleah** |

**Rule: Arabic fā'il → Hebrew qōtēl → Mormon -O-E-.** This is the workhorse transformation for generating mormonized names from Arabic active participles.

**The Verbal Noun (Segolate) Shift:**

Arabic verbal nouns from Form I are often monosyllabic: *ḥamd*, *fatḥ*, *ʿilm*. Hebrew cognates of these are typically **segolate nouns** — originally monosyllabic but expanded to two syllables with an inserted /e/ (segol):

| Arabic VN | Pattern | Hebrew Segolate | Pattern | Mormon |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *ḥamd* "praise" | CaCC | *ḥemed* "delight" | CeCeC | **Hemed** |
| *fatḥ* "opening/victory" | CaCC | *petaḥ* "opening" | CeCaC | **Petah** |
| *ʿilm* "knowledge" | CiCC | — | — | — |
| *ḥukm* "judgment" | CuCC | *ḥokhmāh* "wisdom" (root Ḥ-K-M) | CoCCāC | **Hochmah** |
| *quds* "holiness" | CuCC | *qōdesh* "holiness" | CōCeC | **Codesh** |
| *mulk* "kingdom" | CuCC | *melekh* "king" | CeCeC | **Melech** |

**Rule: Arabic CaCC/CiCC/CuCC → Hebrew CeCeC (segolate) or CōCeC.** The segolate expansion (inserting a vowel to break up the final consonant cluster) is one of Hebrew's most distinctive morphological features. It produces the classic biblical name-shapes: *Melech*, *Petah*, *Codesh*.

#### Form III / No Direct Hebrew Equivalent — Associative/Reciprocal

Arabic Form III (*fā'ala*) means "to do X with/to someone." Hebrew has no exact structural equivalent, though the Piel sometimes covers similar semantic territory.

| | Arabic (Form III) |
|---|---|
| **Pattern** | **fā'ala** (lengthened first vowel) |
| **Example** | *qātala* "he fought (with someone)" (root q-t-l) |

Since there is no Hebrew cognate form, words in this pattern would either be borrowed directly (using the borrowing path) or the meaning would be expressed with a different Hebrew construction.

#### Form IV / Hiphil — Causative

Arabic Form IV (prefixed *a-*) and Hebrew Hiphil (prefixed *hi-*) are both **causative**: "he caused X to happen."

| | Arabic (Form IV) | Hebrew (Hiphil) |
|---|---|---|
| **Pattern** | **af'ala** | **hiqtīl** (perfect) / **yaqtīl** (imperfect) |
| **Perfect 3ms** | *aḥmada* "he praised" (≈ Form I here) | *heḥmīd* (hypothetical) |
| **Vowel mapping** | **a-∅-a** | **he-∅-ī** |
| **Active Participle** | *muḥmid* | *maḥmīd* (hypothetical) |
| **Part. prefix** | **mu-** | **ma-** |

The **Hiphil participle prefix ma-** (actually *maq-tīl*) is important — it's different from the Piel prefix *me-*:

| Arabic Form IV Participle Prefix | Hebrew Hiphil Participle Prefix | Mormon |
|---|---|---|
| **mu-** | **ma-** | **MA-** |

This distinction (Piel *me-* vs. Hiphil *ma-*) is subtle but real in Hebrew. In Mormon transliteration, both become M + vowel, but the vowel quality differs: **Me-** for Piel-type words, **Ma-** for Hiphil-type words.

## Part V: Full Worked Derivation — Putting It All Together

Let's take the Arabic word **Muṣḥaf** (مُصْحَف, "codex, bound copy of the Quran") through every step.

### Step 1: Root Identification

Root: **ص-ح-ف** (ṣ-ḥ-f) — "to write on a page, to compile pages"

### Step 2: Cognate Consonant Mapping

| Arabic Consonant | Proto-Semitic | Hebrew Cognate | Mormon |
|---|---|---|---|
| ص ṣād /sˤ/ | *ṣ́ | צ tsade /ts/ | **Z** |
| ح ḥāʾ /ħ/ | *ḥ | ח ḥet /ħ/ | **H** |
| ف fāʾ /f/ | *p | פ pe /p/ | **PH** (final position) |

Root in Hebrew: **צ-ח-פ** (ts-ḥ-p)

### Step 3: Pattern Identification

Arabic *muṣḥaf* follows the pattern **muf'al** — a place/instrument noun from Form I, meaning "a place/thing where pages are compiled" → "a codex, a bound book."

Hebrew equivalent pattern: **miqṭal** (the standard Hebrew place/instrument noun prefix).

Applying the pattern to the Hebrew root: **mitṣḥāph** → simplified → **mitṣḥāph** is too consonant-heavy. Hebrew would likely produce **matsḥēph** or more naturally **matshēph** (מַצְחֵף) — with the tsade pronounced as the affricate /ts/.

### Step 4: Mormon Transliteration

- מ → M
- צ → Z (Split Rule 3, initial position of root but medial in word)
- ח → H (Split Rule 2)
- פ → PH (Split Rule 6, final position)

Vowels: *ma-ts-ḥē-ph* → MA-Z-HE-PH → **Mazheph**

### Notable Coincidences and Semantic Parallels

Several of these speculative derivations produce striking results that merit comment:

- **Coresh** (Quraish) — The diminutive pattern fu'ail applied to root Q-R-Sh produces exactly the Hebrew form of *Cyrus* (כורש, Kōresh). This is a pure phonological coincidence: the same sound-change rules that map Arabic fu'ail to Hebrew produce an output identical to an existing biblical name. Dhu-l-Qarnain (identified as Cyrus) is already translated as "Cyrus" in the scholarly section — the fact that Quraish independently maps to the same Hebrew word is accidental.

- **Molech** (Mālik) — The active participle fā'il→qōtēl pipeline maps the Quranic guardian angel of hellfire to the KJV name for the fire-deity associated with child sacrifice (Lev 18:21). Both figures are defined by their relationship to fire and judgment. The Hebrew form *mōlēkh* is simply the qōtēl participle of M-L-K "to rule."

- **Elath** (Lāt) — If al-Lāt derives from al-ilāhat "the goddess" (the scholarly consensus), then her name is literally the feminine of *'ēl* "god." Hebrew preserves this as *'ēlat*, attested as a KJV place name (2 Kgs 14:22). The pagan goddess reduces to the feminine form of "God."

- **Nesher** (Nasr) — The eagle-god's name maps perfectly to Hebrew *nesher* (נשר) "eagle/vulture" (Deut 32:11) via the ś-path sibilant shift. This is a genuine cognate hiding in a pagan idol name — the Arabic and Hebrew words both mean "eagle" and descend from the same Proto-Semitic root.

- **Hodeviah** (Ḥudaibiyah) — The treaty site near Makkah transforms into something virtually indistinguishable from KJV *Hodaviah* (1 Chr 9:7). The ḥ→h, ai→ē, and b→v shifts are all individually well-attested; their combination produces a word that reads as naturally biblical as any name in Chronicles.

- **Corezah** (Quraiẓah) — The Jewish tribe of Medina maps to a form strikingly close to NT *Chorazin* (Matt 11:21), the Galilean city cursed by Jesus. The q→c, ai→ē, and ẓ→z shifts are standard.
